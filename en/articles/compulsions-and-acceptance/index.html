Compulsions and acceptance: why the way out of OCD does not lead through fighting every thought

Author: Bc. Václav Kodeš · 20 October 2026

You may know this. An intrusive thought appears and you immediately feel that you have to do something with it. You need to refute it, gain certainty, check the situation or replay everything in your head.

You tell yourself that once you have certainty you will finally calm down. And so you start looking for answers.

But instead of lasting relief further doubts arrive. And with them further urges.

What if there were another way? A way in which you do not have to resolve every thought, remove every feeling and refute every doubt?

This is where we come to one of the important principles of coping with obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD): accepting unpleasant thoughts and feelings without having to react to them with a compulsion.

It is not resignation. It is not that you have to come to terms with everything. And it certainly does not mean you have to believe everything that goes through your head.

It is about learning to distinguish between what is going on inside you and what you actually need to do.

1. What is a compulsion and why does OCD demand it so urgently?

A compulsion is a repeated behaviour or mental act that a person feels an urge to do, often in response to an intrusive thought, anxiety or a feeling of uncertainty.

Its aim is usually to reduce unpleasant feelings or prevent a feared event.

A compulsion does not have to be visible at first sight. It can take many forms. For example:

  • You repeatedly check whether you have locked the door.
  • You ask your partner whether they think everything is all right.
  • You look up information on the internet that is meant to refute your worry.
  • In your head you replay a conversation to find out whether you said something inappropriate.
  • You try to find the right feeling that would confirm that everything is as it should be.
  • You repeat a certain thought or mental procedure until you feel it is right.

At first sight these activities can look like an effort to solve a problem. And sometimes checking or looking up information really is useful.

The difference lies in their function. If you repeat something mainly to gain certainty, to neutralise anxiety or to reach a feeling that everything is all right, it can become part of the compulsive cycle.

A compulsion often brings short-term relief. But that relief can strengthen the belief that next time you must do the same. This creates a vicious circle: intrusive thought → anxiety → compulsion → short-term relief → another doubt.

Acceptance offers a possibility to begin to break this cycle gradually.

2. Acceptance does not mean agreeing with a thought

The word acceptance can be confusing. You may imagine that it means you have to come to terms with your worst fears really coming true.

That is not so.

Acceptance in the context of OCD does not mean agreeing with the content of an intrusive thought. Nor does it mean deciding that it is true.

It means admitting that this thought has just appeared, without having to start fighting against it immediately.

Imagine that it occurs to you: "What if I have hurt someone without realising it?"

OCD can push you to start analysing your memories, looking for evidence and repeatedly reassuring yourself that nothing happened.

Acceptance could look different. You notice the thought. You realise that it causes you anxiety. And instead of dissecting it at once you try to allow that right now you have an unpleasant doubt.

You do not have to refute it. You do not have to believe it. You do not even have to reach a definitive conclusion. You can carry on with what you were doing.

Acceptance does not mean telling yourself that everything will definitely turn out well. It means ceasing to demand that you have to convince yourself of it right now.

3. Why does fighting thoughts often not work?

When an unpleasant thought appears, a natural reaction can be an effort to get rid of it.

You tell yourself you will not think about it. You start to concentrate on something else or try to prove to yourself that it has no meaning.

But the more you watch whether the thought has appeared again, the more attention you may give it.

Another problem can arise: you start to fear not only the original thought but also that it will appear again. And when it comes back you may feel you have failed at something.

In OCD another layer of difficulty can therefore develop. The person tries to remove not only the doubt itself but also the anxiety, uncertainty and unpleasant feelings that accompany it.

The result can be endless inner negotiation:

  • "Why did that occur to me?"
  • "What if it means something?"
  • "How do I get rid of this thought?"
  • "Why do I keep thinking about it?"

Acceptance offers a different approach: you do not have to remove the thought in order to carry on with your life.

It can appear and go away again. It can return. And you can gradually learn that its presence does not require an automatic reaction.

4. How to accept anxiety when it is really strong?

This is one of the hardest parts.

When anxiety is mild it can be fairly easy to tell yourself you do not have to deal with it. But when it reaches high intensity the situation looks completely different.

The body is tense, thoughts keep returning and the need to carry out a compulsion can be very strong. You may feel that until you do something you will not be able to carry on with your ordinary day.

Acceptance does not mean that you must feel calm. Nor does it mean that you should suppress anxiety or persuade yourself that nothing is wrong.

You can simply notice:

  • "Right now I feel anxiety."
  • "I have a strong urge to make sure."
  • "It is hard for me to let this doubt be."

You do not have to add explanations to these sentences or look for a way to calm down immediately.

You can allow the unpleasant feeling to exist and at the same time decide that you will not automatically follow every urge.

Sometimes the anxiety gradually weakens. At other times it will persist for some time or rise again. Neither in itself means that you have failed.

Acceptance is not a technique that has to remove anxiety immediately. It is a way of relating to it without having to adapt your behaviour to every unpleasant feeling.

5. A practical example: when you feel a need to make sure again

Imagine that you have sent a work e-mail. Soon afterwards a doubt appears: "What if I wrote something inappropriate?"

You open the sent message and read it. Then again. You examine individual sentences and wonder how the other person might have interpreted them. Finally you ask a colleague whether the e-mail looks normal.

For a moment you feel relief. But within a few minutes another doubt appears: "And what if my colleague did not notice the problematic part?" The whole process starts again.

What could a reaction based on acceptance look like?

First you notice the urge to check the e-mail again. Then you decide that you will not carry on with repeated checking merely because of anxiety.

Perhaps you still feel uncertainty. Perhaps you feel like opening the message once more. Even so you go back to work.

You do not tell yourself that the e-mail was definitely perfect. Nor do you try to prove that you did nothing wrong. You merely admit that you cannot have absolute certainty about how every person will understand every sentence.

And if there is a specific, practically solvable mistake, you can put it right in the ordinary way. Acceptance is not a ban on sensible action. It is about recognising the difference between useful checking and repetition that mainly serves to neutralise anxiety.

6. What if acceptance itself becomes another compulsion?

This is an important point that is sometimes forgotten.

A person with OCD may start to use acceptance as another way to gain certainty or to remove unpleasant feelings.

They may try to accept a thought perfectly. They repeat certain sentences and check whether they have really accepted it yet. For example:

  • "I must accept this thought."
  • "Am I accepting it correctly?"
  • "Why do I still feel anxiety when I should already be accepting?"
  • "What if I have not understood acceptance correctly?"

And an originally helpful approach can become another mental ritual.

Acceptance is not a task you have to complete without a mistake. You do not have to reach perfect inner calm. You do not have to repeat the right sentences. You do not have to check whether you have already made peace with your thought.

You can simply notice that you are trying to do something perfectly, and gradually learn to let go of that effort.

Sometimes you will be able to let the thought be. At other times you will get tangled in analysing again. That is part of the learning process, not proof that acceptance does not work.

7. Acceptance and ritual prevention: how are they connected?

Acceptance is not the only method of working with OCD, but it fits well with the principles of cognitive behavioural therapy and especially exposure and response prevention (ERP).

In ERP a person gradually and in a planned way meets situations that trigger obsessive anxiety and learns not to carry out the usual compulsions.

For example, someone who repeatedly checks a locked door can, as part of suitably set-up treatment, learn to leave after an ordinary check without going back because of further doubts.

The point is not to prove to yourself that nothing bad will happen. It is a change in your relationship to uncertainty and to the urge to check.

Acceptance can be useful in this process because it helps a person allow unpleasant feelings without having to neutralise them immediately.

It is important to proceed in a measured way and to distinguish between compulsive behaviour and ordinary safety precautions. If OCD significantly affects everyday life, it is advisable to plan treatment with a professional who has experience with OCD and ERP.

8. You do not have to resolve every doubt in order to carry on

Perhaps for a long time you believed that calm will come only once you have all the answers.

And so you looked for certainty. You checked. You analysed. You asked others.

Perhaps these activities repeatedly brought you relief, but the doubts came back anyway.

Acceptance offers a different possibility.

You do not have to have every thought resolved in order to go for a walk, spend time with your family, work or do something that has meaning for you.

You do not have to get rid of anxiety first in order to carry on with your day. And you do not even have to wait until you feel you have accepted everything correctly.

You can notice that a doubt has appeared. You can feel discomfort. And you can still decide to give your attention to what is important to you.

It does not mean that OCD will stop speaking up overnight. Change may take time, patience and sometimes professional support.

Every moment when you begin to recognise an urge and look for a different reaction, however, can be an opportunity to learn.

In conclusion: acceptance is not giving up

Acceptance does not mean that you give up the effort to feel better.

It means that you gradually give up the endless fight for absolute certainty.

You do not have to agree with every thought. You do not have to believe it. And you do not have to refute it at all costs either.

You can let it be a thought.

You can allow that anxiety is present right now without having to check or fix something immediately.

And you can carry on with life even when a certain amount of uncertainty stays in it.

Freedom from OCD does not have to begin at the moment unpleasant thoughts stop coming. It can begin at the moment you learn that you do not have to react to every one of them.

That is something you can learn gradually. Without perfection, without rushing and without demanding that you manage everything at once.

The article is informative and does not replace professional care. If OCD significantly affects your everyday life, a psychotherapist with experience of treating OCD, especially cognitive behavioural therapy and the ERP method, can help.
