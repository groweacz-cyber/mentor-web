Types of OCD: 9 types, their symptoms and treatment options

Author: Bc. Václav Kodeš · 16 October 2026

Obsessive-compulsive disorder, known by the abbreviation OCD, can take many different forms. It does not always show itself as repeated hand washing or checking doors. In some people intrusive thoughts predominate, in others constant doubts, fear of illness or a need to obtain reassurance.

What types of OCD exist, how to recognise their typical manifestations and how is obsessive-compulsive disorder treated? In this article we look at nine common thematic forms of OCD, explain the differences between obsessions and compulsions and present the options for professional help.

What is OCD, or obsessive-compulsive disorder?

OCD is an abbreviation of the English name obsessive-compulsive disorder. In Czech the term obsedantně-kompulzivní porucha is used.

It is a mental disorder characterised by obsessions, compulsions or a combination of both.

Obsessions are repeated intrusive thoughts, images, impulses or doubts that cause the person anxiety or marked discomfort.

Compulsions are repeated actions or mental acts by which a person tries to reduce anxiety, prevent a feared event or gain a feeling of certainty.

Compulsions therefore need not include only visible actions such as hand washing or checking a lock. They can also take the form of repeated thinking, analysing your own feelings, replaying memories or seeking reassurance.

A typical cycle may look like this:

  1. An intrusive thought or doubt appears.
  2. The person starts to feel anxiety and a need to resolve the situation.
  3. They try to gain certainty through checking, analysing or reassurance.
  4. Short-term relief arrives.
  5. The doubt returns and the person feels a need to repeat the whole process.

This mechanism can gradually interfere with work, relationships and everyday life.

Not every intrusive thought or occasional check means OCD. To make a diagnosis it is important to assess the overall symptoms, their intensity, how much time they take and their impact on the person's functioning.

What types of OCD exist?

OCD can focus on different themes. Common thematic forms include fear of contamination, checking, worries about health, relationship doubts, intrusive thoughts about harming others and a need for symmetry.

It is important to know that the following labels are mainly thematic groups of symptoms. They are not nine separate official diagnoses. One person can have several of these manifestations at the same time and their focus can change over time.

1. Contamination OCD: fear of germs, dirt and contamination

Contamination OCD is marked by intrusive fears of dirt, germs, viruses, chemicals or other potentially harmful substances.

A person may fear that they have touched a contaminated object, carried dirt home or endangered their own health or the health of loved ones.

Possible symptoms include:

  • excessive hand washing and showering
  • repeated cleaning of objects and the home
  • avoiding certain places or objects
  • repeated changing of clothes and washing
  • a need to follow exactly set hygiene procedures

The problem need not lie only in the fear of dirt. The person may also feel that they can never be sufficiently certain that they are really clean or safe.

2. Checking OCD: a constant need to verify everything

Checking OCD shows itself as repeated doubts about whether a person has done something correctly, forgotten something or caused a dangerous situation.

They may repeatedly check a lock, a cooker, electrical appliances or a sent message, even though they have already verified the thing.

Possible symptoms include:

  • repeated checking of doors and appliances
  • returning to a place to verify an earlier action
  • repeated reading of e-mails out of fear of a mistake
  • doubts about their own memories
  • a need to obtain confirmation that everything is all right

Checking brings short-term relief, but the doubts can return. The person then gets a feeling that they have to check again.

3. Health OCD: an intrusive fear of serious illness

In OCD focused on health, obsessive doubts concern possible illness or health symptoms.

A person may repeatedly worry that they suffer from a serious illness, have overlooked an important symptom or that the doctor did not find something.

Possible symptoms include:

  • constantly searching for symptoms on the internet
  • repeatedly monitoring bodily sensations
  • comparing their own symptoms with descriptions of diseases
  • repeatedly seeking reassurance
  • doubting the results of examinations
  • mentally analysing what a particular symptom might mean

A person may feel relief after reading reassuring information or receiving reassurance. Later, however, another doubt may appear and a need to start looking for answers again.

It is important to distinguish between OCD focused on health and health anxiety, also called illness anxiety. These difficulties can overlap but they are not identical. Worries about health alone do not automatically mean OCD.

4. Pure O: OCD dominated by intrusive thoughts

Pure O is an informal term for a form of OCD in which obsessions predominate and compulsions may not be outwardly visible.

A person may spend a long time thinking about their thoughts, searching for their meaning or checking their own feelings.

Possible symptoms include:

  • repeatedly analysing intrusive thoughts
  • replaying past situations
  • trying to gain certainty about what a particular thought means
  • checking their own emotions and reactions
  • repeating reassuring sentences in their head
  • searching for information meant to disprove the worries

For example, an unpleasant thought occurs to someone and they start to wonder why it appeared, whether it says something about their personality and whether it could mean something dangerous. The more they try to reach definitive certainty, the more doubts may arise.

The label Pure O is somewhat misleading, because compulsions can occur in these cases too. They often take the form of mental rituals that those around the person may not notice at all.

5. Relationship OCD (ROCD): doubts about a partner and the relationship

Relationship OCD, abbreviated ROCD, involves obsessive doubts connected with a romantic relationship.

A person may repeatedly examine whether they truly love their partner, whether the relationship is right or whether they have chosen the right person.

Possible symptoms include:

  • constantly analysing their own feelings
  • doubts about whether the relationship is right
  • comparing the partner with other people
  • repeatedly looking for evidence for and against the relationship
  • a need for reassurance
  • monitoring their own emotions when in contact with the partner

Ordinary questions about a relationship are not in themselves a sign of OCD. What matters is whether the doubts turn into a repetitive process that causes the person marked distress and limits their everyday life.

6. OCD focused on harm: fear of losing control

This form of OCD can show itself as intrusive images of harming someone, causing an accident or losing control of one's actions.

Such thoughts can be very disturbing, especially when they conflict with the person's values.

Possible symptoms include:

  • repeatedly replaying situations in which an accident could happen
  • checking their own intentions
  • trying to gain certainty that they are not dangerous
  • avoiding certain situations
  • repeatedly analysing their own thoughts

The content of an intrusive thought alone is not enough to determine a diagnosis. The overall context and a professional assessment of the difficulties are decisive.

7. Sexual OCD: unwanted sexual thoughts and doubts

Sexual OCD can show itself as intrusive sexual images, unwanted thoughts or doubts about one's own desires and identity.

A person may repeatedly examine what their thoughts mean, check their reactions or seek reassurance about what they truly feel.

Possible symptoms include:

  • repeatedly analysing sexual thoughts
  • checking bodily reactions
  • examining their own feelings
  • searching for information and reassurance
  • trying to gain absolute certainty about their own preferences

The mere presence of a thought does not automatically mean a desire or an intention. At the same time, a diagnosis cannot be made merely because certain thoughts are unpleasant or unwanted.

8. Moral and religious OCD: fear of mistakes and guilt

The moral and religious form of OCD is also called scrupulosity.

A person may excessively worry that they have done something wrong, hurt someone or violated important moral or religious principles.

Possible symptoms include:

  • repeatedly thinking about past situations
  • excessive feelings of guilt
  • repeated examination of conscience
  • a need to reassure themselves that they have done nothing wrong
  • compulsive prayers or repeating certain acts
  • trying to obtain a definitive answer to moral questions

A person may feel that they have to examine every decision thoroughly to be sure they acted correctly.

9. OCD focused on symmetry, order and a feeling of completeness

This form of OCD is connected with a need for symmetry, exact arrangement or a feeling that something has been completed in the right way.

A person may repeat certain movements, straighten objects or follow specific rules until they feel that everything is in order.

Possible symptoms include:

  • repeatedly straightening objects
  • a need for symmetrical arrangement
  • repeating movements and actions
  • counting or following certain rules
  • being unable to stop until a feeling of completeness arrives

The main problem is not always fear of a specific danger. Sometimes an unpleasant feeling that something is not right or not finished predominates.

Ordinary carefulness, a sense of order or perfectionism do not in themselves mean OCD.

How to recognise OCD? Thoughts alone are not decisive

One important sign of OCD can be a repeating cycle of intrusive thoughts and compulsive reactions.

For example, a person feels a doubt, starts to analyse the situation, looks for reassurance and calms down for a while. But as soon as a new question appears, the whole process repeats.

The problem may be that the person cannot simply let uncertainty be. They feel they must resolve the doubt until they have complete certainty.

A professional assessment is advisable especially when:

  • thoughts or rituals take up a considerable part of the day,
  • the person repeatedly tries to gain certainty but the relief does not last,
  • they limit ordinary activities because of their worries,
  • they avoid situations that did not bother them before,
  • the difficulties interfere with work, relationships or sleep.

No single symptom on its own confirms a diagnosis. If someone suspects OCD, they can turn to a psychologist, psychotherapist or psychiatrist with experience of this disorder.

How is OCD treated?

The main options for treating OCD include psychotherapy and, in some cases, medication.

Cognitive behavioural therapy and ERP

Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) is among the established psychotherapeutic approaches in the treatment of OCD.

An important part of it can be exposure and response prevention (ERP). In this method the person gradually learns to face situations that provoke anxiety and to limit the compulsive reactions that keep their difficulties going.

The aim is not to gain a hundred percent certainty that nothing bad will happen. The aim is to learn to handle uncertainty without having to constantly check, analyse or seek reassurance.

I-CBT: therapy focused on obsessive doubts

I-CBT (Inference-Based Cognitive Behavioral Therapy) is a therapeutic approach that focuses on the process by which obsessive doubts arise.

Among other things it works with the distinction between what a person actually observes or knows and what they merely imagine as a possible scenario.

For example, a person may start to doubt their health even though they have no convincing current evidence for their worry. They then create further hypothetical scenarios and try to resolve them all.

I-CBT helps to recognise this way of thinking and to work with how obsessive doubts are formed.

It is a specific therapeutic approach that differs from ERP in its focus. ERP currently has a broader evidence base and is among the standard recommended procedures in the treatment of OCD.

Medication in the treatment of OCD

In some cases the treatment can also include medication, especially antidepressants from the SSRI group.

A doctor decides whether medication is suitable according to the severity of the difficulties, overall health and other individual circumstances.

Properly chosen treatment can help reduce the intensity of symptoms and improve quality of life.

Frequently asked questions about types of OCD

What are the most common types of OCD?

Common thematic forms of OCD include contamination OCD, checking OCD, obsessive doubts focused on health, Pure O, relationship OCD, OCD focused on harming others, sexual OCD, moral OCD and OCD connected with symmetry or a feeling of completeness. These are not, however, separate official diagnostic categories. Individual manifestations can overlap.

What is Pure O?

Pure O is an informal term for OCD dominated by intrusive thoughts and mainly mental compulsions. A person may repeatedly analyse their thoughts, check their feelings or seek reassurance without those around them noticing any obvious rituals.

Can OCD show itself only in the head?

Yes. Compulsions do not have to be visible. They can take the form of repeated thinking, replaying memories, mental checking or an effort to obtain certainty. However, that alone is not enough to determine a diagnosis.

Is health anxiety the same as OCD?

No. Health anxiety and OCD focused on health can overlap, but they are different clinical concepts. To tell them apart it is important to assess the nature of the worries, the repeated reactions and the person's overall functioning.

Can you have more than one type of OCD at the same time?

Yes. One person can, for example, have worries about health, a need to repeatedly check certain situations and intrusive thoughts at the same time. The thematic focus of OCD can also change over time.

Can OCD be cured?

OCD can be treated effectively. The main options include cognitive behavioural therapy, especially ERP, and, according to individual needs, medication. Results of treatment differ and the suitable approach is best determined with a professional.

Conclusion: OCD takes different forms but works through similar mechanisms

Obsessive-compulsive disorder does not have to show itself only as hand washing or checking objects. It can also include intrusive thoughts, repeated doubts, fear of illness or a compulsive need to obtain reassurance.

The individual thematic forms of OCD can overlap and their focus can change over a lifetime. To understand the difficulties it is therefore important to notice not only the content of the thoughts but also how the person reacts to them.

OCD is not a matter of weak willpower. If compulsive thoughts and rituals limit a person in everyday life, it makes sense to seek professional help.

A useful first step is to understand your own patterns of experience and find out what professional help is available.
