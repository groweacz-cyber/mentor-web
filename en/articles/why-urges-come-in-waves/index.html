Why urges come in waves: you do not have to react to every urge

Author: Bc. Václav Kodeš · 16 October 2026

You may know this. A moment ago you felt quite well, but suddenly an intrusive thought, a doubt or an unpleasant feeling appears. Anxiety begins to rise and with it an urge to do something.

You need to make sure, check something, look up information or replay a certain situation in your head.

The urge is strong. You feel you must act now or the anxiety will get even worse.

And then something interesting happens. After a while the intensity of the urge starts to fall. Sometimes fairly quickly, sometimes gradually. Occasionally it comes back again.

Why does this happen? And what can you do when the next wave of urges is flooding you right now?

1. OCD does not always come with the same intensity

Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) can be very changeable. Sometimes intrusive thoughts are almost unnoticeable, at other times they take up a large part of the day.

The intensity of urges can fluctuate depending on many factors, for example stress, tiredness, lack of sleep or situations that make us feel uncertain.

Sometimes even a seemingly trivial thing can set off anxiety. A thought, a bodily sensation, a memory or a certain situation. The brain judges the stimulus as potentially important and a need to do something appears.

You may feel that this time the urge is stronger than ever. That you must resolve it immediately. That until you get an answer you will not be able to function normally.

But the intensity of an urge changes over time. What feels unbearable right now may not have the same strength in ten minutes.

A strong urge is not an order you must obey. It is an unpleasant experience that you can gradually learn to handle differently.

2. Why does an urge resemble a wave?

Imagine the sea. A wave first begins to rise. It gradually gains strength, reaches a peak and finally dissolves.

A wave of anxiety and urges can run in a similar way.

First comes a trigger. For example a doubt about whether you did something correctly.

Then anxiety appears. You start to analyse the situation and look for a way to get rid of the unpleasant feeling.

The urge grows. You feel a need to check, to ask or to gain certainty.

If you do not obey the urge, the anxiety may persist for some time. It may not drop immediately. Gradually, though, it can start to change and weaken.

Sometimes another wave appears. At other times the situation calms down and you can return to what you were doing.

It is important to know that the course is not always the same. Some waves fade quickly, others last longer and sometimes the urge returns repeatedly.

But you do not have to wait until the wave has completely disappeared before you can carry on with your life.

3. Why do we feel that we must obey the urge immediately?

When OCD triggers strong anxiety, the brain tries to find a route to relief. It may whisper to you:

  • "You had better check it once more."
  • "What if you overlook something this time?"
  • "You have to be sure."
  • "Until you resolve this you cannot move on."
  • "What if something happens if you do not do it now?"

These thoughts can seem urgent and convincing.

If you react to them with a compulsion, for example checking or reassurance, you may feel relief. The brain thus learns that carrying out a ritual helps reduce unpleasant feelings.

This, however, can strengthen the link between the doubt and the need to react to it. When a similar situation appears next time, the urge may come again.

That is why, in coping with OCD, it is important not only to work with the content of thoughts but also to change the way we react to them.

The aim is not to make sure the urge never appears. The aim is to gradually learn that you do not have to answer it every time with checking, a ritual or seeking reassurance.

4. What happens when you do not obey the urge?

This may be one of the hardest parts of the whole process.

When you decide not to carry out the compulsion, you may feel that you cannot bear the anxiety. You may be nervous, restless and have a strong need to change the situation.

Perhaps you expect to feel relief straight away when you do not obey the urge. That may not happen, though.

The anxiety may at first stay the same, get stronger or fluctuate. You may feel that nothing is changing and that your effort is pointless.

Even so you can gain a new experience: you do not have to automatically deal with an unpleasant feeling with a ritual.

Gradually you may find that you can carry on with ordinary activities even when you do not have complete certainty and the anxiety has not yet subsided.

This principle is, among other things, the basis of exposure and response prevention (ERP), which is used in the treatment of OCD.

It is not about forcing yourself at all costs or exposing yourself to real danger. It is about thoughtful, gradual learning of different reactions to situations that trigger obsessive anxiety.

If OCD limits you markedly, it can be worthwhile to work on it with a therapist who has experience with this method.

5. What can make waves of urges stronger?

Some circumstances can make an urge appear more often or make it harder to cope with.

Stress and overload. When you have a lot on, it can be harder to bear uncertainty and resist the urge.

Lack of sleep and tiredness. Tiredness can impair the ability to regulate attention and to respond to unpleasant thoughts with some distance.

Trying to get a hundred percent certainty. The more you try to settle every doubt definitively, the more room you can give to further analysing and checking.

Repeated reassurance. When you repeatedly ask others, look up information or check your own feelings, the need to do it again can grow stronger.

Constant monitoring of your own state. If you keep checking whether the anxiety has subsided yet, your attention may return again and again to what is troubling you.

This of course does not mean that you must remove all stressors or stop monitoring your health. The point is to recognise when ordinary effort to look after yourself turns into repeated behaviour motivated by a need to get certainty.

6. How to cope with a wave of urges in practice?

When the urge comes next time you do not need a complicated plan ready. You can start with a few simple steps.

1. Name what is happening.

Instead of reacting immediately, notice: "An urge has appeared. I have a need to make sure." You do not have to decide whether your thought is true, or refute it.

2. Try not to obey the urge for a while.

If you have a need to check something or ask again, you can try not reacting to that need. You do not have to promise yourself that you will never do it again. Focus on your present reaction.

3. Allow the unpleasant feeling to exist.

You do not have to remove the anxiety immediately. You can be nervous and at the same time carry on with an ordinary activity. The aim is not to wait and watch for when the anxiety finally disappears. It is about giving it room without having to adapt all your behaviour to it.

4. Return to what matters to you.

Carry on with work, prepare a meal, spend time with your family or return to a favourite activity. Not in order to forcibly drive the urge away, but because your life does not have to depend on whether you feel completely calm right now.

5. Do not judge your success by how fast the anxiety fades.

You may feel relief within a few minutes. The urge may come back several times. That alone does not mean you have done something wrong. Success can also be that this time you did not react automatically, even though it was unpleasant.

7. What if the urge comes back again?

You may feel that when the same doubt appears again you have to start the whole process from the beginning. That is not necessary.

The return of an urge does not mean that your earlier effort was pointless. OCD can come in repeated waves and some situations can be harder than others.

You do not have to analyse every new wave, compare it with the previous one or find out why it appeared right now.

You can simply notice that the urge has come back and decide again how you want to react to it.

Sometimes it will be easy. At other times you will need more support.

It is important not to try to turn every wave into another problem that you have to solve perfectly.

8. You do not have to wait for calm in order to live

One of the most important things you can learn in coping with OCD is to distinguish between what you feel and what you must do.

You can feel anxiety without having to check. You can have a doubt without having to look for an answer. You can feel an urge without having to obey it.

This does not mean you will always manage it. And it does not mean the urge will weaken immediately.

It means you are gradually creating room for a different reaction. And it is in this room that your relationship with OCD can begin to change.

You do not have to wait until you are completely calm to go out, spend time with your family or do something that is important to you.

You can live even in the presence of uncertainty.

An urge can come like a wave. You do not have to stop it, resolve it or remove it immediately. You can gradually learn to let it pass without it deciding for you what you do next.

And if you do not manage it today, it does not mean you have failed. Every step towards a different way of reacting counts.
