When OCD Lives Next to You

Do you live with a person who has OCD, or look after someone like that? You may be asking how to help, while feeling that everything you try does not help or even makes it worse. You are not alone in this.

Bc. Václav Kodeš

Peer mentor with personal experience of OCD

Last updated: 3 November 2026

OCD is often kept going even by well-meant help: when we check for the person, answer the same questions again and again, or avoid things that trigger fear. From the outside it looks like kindness, but in the long run it feeds the fear. It can be done differently, without blaming yourself or your loved one.

At the seminar "When OCD Lives Next to You", from the perspective of someone who knows OCD from their own life, I will show you what helps and what harms and how to keep your own life while doing it. I am not a psychologist or therapist, and I do not provide family therapy or advice on your specific case.

What accommodation is

Accommodation is when a loved one changes their behaviour because of OCD: answers reassurance questions, avoids triggers, handles things for the person or takes part in rituals. They do it out of love and out of a wish to ease things.

In the short term it works. The tension eases and the household is calm. In the long term, though, OCD grows, because it is confirmed that without the ritual something would be wrong. Studies have also repeatedly found a link between the degree of accommodation and the severity of symptoms (Lebowitz et al., 2012).

Why reassurance does not help

The question "Is it really all right?" needs fresh reassurance every time. The more answers are given, the more the need to ask returns. The article on reassurance covers this in more detail.

What to do instead

  • Acknowledge the feeling, not the content. Say that you can see they are afraid and that you will stay with them. Do not answer the "what if" question.
  • Agree in advance and calmly. Agreeing is hard only at the moment of greatest anxiety. Changes made without agreement tend to be hard for both of you.
  • Change gradually. You do not have to stop overnight. It is more important to agree on a direction and a pace, ideally with the support of a professional.
  • Leave the responsibility with them. Do not take over rituals or decisions for them.

Sample responses

A few sentences that can be used instead of reassurance. They serve as inspiration, not as a script that works for everything. You can fine-tune them with your loved one and with a professional.

  • "I can see you are afraid. I am not going to give you an answer right now, but I am here with you."
  • "I understand it is hard right now. I believe you can bear it."
  • "I know it is unpleasant. Let us get through it together for a while."

What this page is not

This is not a guide to dealing with OCD at home. Professional care is led by a professional, not by a partner, parent or friend. The role of a loved one is to support, not to lead professional care.

You deserve support too

Living alongside OCD can be exhausting. You may feel helpless, irritated or guilty, and that is all right. Talk about it with someone you trust, and if it is too much, look for support for yourself as well. The boundaries you set are not selfish.

You can look for support for yourself with a general practitioner, a psychotherapist or a mental health centre. An overview of verified organisations is in the section Where next: professional care. In an acute crisis call 112, 155 or the Czech first psychological help line 116 123.

When to seek a professional

Contact a doctor or another professional if your worries and rituals take up a lot of time, limit your work, relationships or sleep, or if your condition is getting worse. Do not wait until it gets worse.

In an acute crisis or with thoughts of suicide or self-harm, call 112, 155 or the Czech first psychological help line 116 123. More on the page Help now.
