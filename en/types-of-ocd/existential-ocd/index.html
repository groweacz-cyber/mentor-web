Existential OCD

Existential OCD is a form of OCD in which philosophical questions about meaning, reality, existence or death turn into an urge to have a certain answer to them.

What it is

Being interested in the big questions is human. In existential OCD, though, the question "What if it has no meaning?" or "What if nothing is real?" turns into an obsession: it causes anxiety and the person tries to resolve it by thinking, reading and looking for arguments. But a certain answer does not exist, so the search repeats.

The difference is not in the topic, but in what the person does with it and how much room it takes up in life. If the doubts go round and round and take up a lot of time, it may be OCD. Similar feelings may also appear with depersonalisation or derealisation. A professional can tell them apart.

What obsessions and compulsions look like

Obsessions (examples)

  • What if nothing has meaning?
  • What if the world I perceive is not real?
  • Fear of my own death or of what comes after it.
  • What if I can never be sure that I exist?

Compulsions (examples)

  • Long ruminating and searching for the "right" answer.
  • Reading philosophy, articles or discussions in the hope of finding certainty.
  • Reality testing: checking whether something is "real".
  • Asking others or artificial intelligence what they think about it.
  • Avoiding topics that trigger the question (films, news, conversations).

Common myths

Myth: It is enough to find the right answer.
Reality: For the big questions a certain answer does not exist. Looking for certainty is exactly the compulsion that keeps the loop going.
Myth: Only a deep thinker works on such questions.
Reality: Reflecting on meaning is human and enriching. In OCD, though, the question causes anxiety, takes up a great deal of time and brings no peace, only further questions.
Myth: If I do not think about it, the questions will disappear.
Reality: Suppression usually brings the questions back. A direction different from fighting is to accept that I do not have to have an answer to some questions.

How it is generally addressed by a professional

Professionals (a psychiatrist, clinical psychologist, psychotherapist) usually work with OCD using methods such as exposure and response prevention (ERP), or possibly inference-based cognitive behavioural therapy (I-CBT). A professional also determines the pace, the plan and possibly medication. This page is not a guide to carrying out such procedures on your own at home.

How to find professional help is summarised on the page Help now and in the section Where next: professional care.

Related types

  • Pure O: OCD with obsessions and rituals taking place mainly in the head, invisible from outside.
  • Fear of going mad: Worry about losing control or sanity.
  • Magical thinking: A belief that a thought, number or ritual influences whether something happens.
  • Scrupulosity: Excessive doubts about whether I am morally in order or whether I have sinned.
  • Relationship OCD: Constant doubts about the relationship, the partner or my own feelings.
  • Confession OCD: An urge to confess or to check whether I have done something wrong.

When to seek a professional

Contact a doctor or another professional if your worries and rituals take up a lot of time, limit your work, relationships or sleep, or if your condition is getting worse. Do not wait until it gets worse.

In an acute crisis or with thoughts of suicide or self-harm, call 112, 155 or the Czech first psychological help line 116 123. More on the page Help now.
