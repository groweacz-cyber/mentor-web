Health OCD

Health OCD is a form of OCD in which the obsession revolves around illness: a worry that I have or will get some serious disease, and an endless search for certainty that I am healthy.

Bc. Václav Kodeš

Peer mentor with personal experience of OCD

Last updated: 23 October 2026

What it is

In health OCD, a bodily sensation, news about an illness or merely the thought of one triggers strong fear. The thought "what if it is something serious?" cannot be let go, and the person does everything to check that they are all right. Checking, however, brings only short relief and the doubts come back, often stronger.

Health worries in themselves are not OCD. Caring about your health is normal. We begin to think about OCD when worries and checks take up a lot of time, are exhausting and limit everyday life. Whether it is OCD or another kind of difficulty, only a professional can tell. This website diagnoses nothing.

Important: OCD does not rule out a real illness. If you have a new or persisting symptom, it belongs with a doctor.

What obsessions and compulsions look like

Obsessions (examples)

  • What if that change on my skin means the start of a serious illness?
  • What if I overlooked a symptom that I should have taken seriously?
  • Intrusive images of my own illness or of a hospital room.
  • A fear that my doctor did not tell me something or overlooked something.

Compulsions (examples)

  • Repeatedly feeling and examining the body, measuring blood pressure, pulse or temperature.
  • Looking up symptoms on the internet or with artificial intelligence.
  • Repeated visits to the doctor or requests for further tests "just in case".
  • Reassurance from loved ones ("Don't I look ill?").
  • Mental reviewing: going through symptoms in my head and comparing them with the description of a disease.
  • Avoiding medical news, hospitals or people who are ill.

Common myths

Myth: It is just excessive caution.
Reality: Reasonable care of your health has its limits and ends once the task is dealt with. Health OCD goes round and round, and every further check increases the need for another check.
Myth: If I get examined one more time, I will finally calm down.
Reality: The relief is short and the doubt returns. Another examination "for peace of mind" usually strengthens the loop. When an examination is genuinely appropriate is for a doctor to judge.
Myth: Googling symptoms is responsible.
Reality: Looking up symptoms on the internet is often one of the compulsions. The more you search, the more "evidence" for the worry you find.
Myth: I have health OCD, so nothing can be wrong with me.
Reality: That cannot be claimed, and nobody can give such certainty, neither I nor this website. OCD and a real illness can exist side by side. That is why health questions are answered by a doctor.

How it is generally addressed by a professional

Professionals (a psychiatrist, clinical psychologist, psychotherapist) usually work with OCD using methods such as exposure and response prevention (ERP), or possibly inference-based cognitive behavioural therapy (I-CBT). A professional also determines the pace, the plan and possibly medication. This page is not a guide to carrying out such procedures on your own at home.

How to find professional help is summarised on the page Help now and in the section Where next: professional care.

With health worries it is good to have, alongside a psychiatrist or psychologist, a general practitioner who assesses physical complaints.

How peer support can help

Related types

  • Checking: Repeated checking of locks, appliances and my own actions out of fear that I will cause harm.
  • Sensorimotor OCD: Constant awareness of breathing, blinking, swallowing or other bodily sensations.
  • Fear of going mad: Worry about losing control or sanity.
  • Pure O: OCD with obsessions and rituals taking place mainly in the head, invisible from outside.
  • Emetophobic OCD: Fear of vomiting and rituals meant to prevent vomiting.

When to seek a professional

Contact a doctor or another professional if your worries and rituals take up a lot of time, limit your work, relationships or sleep, or if your condition is getting worse. Do not wait until it gets worse.

In an acute crisis or with thoughts of suicide or self-harm, call 112, 155 or the Czech first psychological help line 116 123. More on the page Help now.
